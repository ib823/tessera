# Putusan Asing Tanpa Izin

On Thursday the nineteenth of November, at ten in the morning in The Hague and five in the afternoon in Kuala Lumpur, the International Court of Justice gave birth.

It had been a long pregnancy. Seven years, from the day The Gambia filed. Thousands of pages of pleadings. A delivery room in the Peace Palace with chandeliers the size of village wells, and judges in robes so long that interns had to carry the hems down the corridor like bridesmaids. When the baby came it was large, pale and made entirely of paper, and it screamed, in both official languages, the only two words the World Court is allowed to scream.

FINAL. BINDING.

The registrar held it up for the cameras. "Congratulations," he said. "It is a judgment. There is no appeal."

"Does it have legs?" asked a journalist.

The registrar checked. "No legs."

"Arms?"

"No arms. No police, no army, no bailiff, no bicycle. It is final and binding, and it cannot walk. Someone must carry it."

"Who?"

"In theory, its father." Everyone turned to look at the Security Council, where five men sat on one sofa, each gripping a remote control marked VETO, arguing about the channel. Nobody had changed it since 1945.

The registrar handed the baby to the nearest postman.

---

The postman flew it to Naypyidaw, a capital city built in the jungle with twenty-lane highways for nobody, and found the General at home being fitted for more medals. The General had so many medals that his chest had begun to sag forward under the weight, and two aides walked ahead of him at all times holding it up, like the train of a wedding dress worn backwards.

"Delivery," said the postman. "About the Rohingya."

The General read the envelope and handed it back.

"There is no such thing," he said.

"No such thing as the case?"

"No such thing as the word. We have never used the word. If there is no word, there are no people. If there are no people, nothing happened to them. If nothing happened to them, this envelope is addressed to nobody." He took out a rubber stamp. RETURN TO SENDER. ADDRESSEE DOES NOT EXIST.

The postman had to admit it was an efficient system. It needed no court, no prison and no paperwork, only a dictionary with one page torn out. The aides lowered the General's chest onto a velvet cushion, and he went to lunch.

---

The postman took the judgment to Jakarta, where ASEAN lives.

ASEAN's secretariat had a glass case, lit from below. Inside it lay the Five-Point Consensus of 2021, a document about Myanmar agreed by every member, consulted at every summit since and carried out at none. Officials in pressed shirts approached it in single file, bowed, and backed away. Above the case, in gold, ran the founding doctrine: NON-INTERFERENCE. Beneath it, in smaller letters: IN THE AFFAIRS OF ANYONE WHO MIGHT ONE DAY INTERFERE IN OURS.

"I have a judgment," said the postman. "It needs enforcing."

"Wonderful," said ASEAN. "We will respond by consensus."

"How long does that take?"

"No time at all, once all eleven members agree."

"All eleven?"

"Including Myanmar."

The postman worked it through. "So Myanmar has a veto over what ASEAN thinks about Myanmar."

"That," said ASEAN, glowing, "is what makes us a family."

In ASEAN, consensus is a physical act. The document is passed round the table, every member must chew it and swallow it, and the meeting cannot end until eleven stomachs hold it down. Ten members chewed. Ten members swallowed, with a range of expressions. In the eleventh chair sat Myanmar's representative. He chewed for a long time, made the face of a cat with a fishbone, and vomited the judgment onto the table.

Everyone looked at the vomit.

"I propose," said the Chair, "that we describe this as constructive engagement."

It was agreed by consensus.

---

The last stop was Kuala Lumpur, because everybody knew Malaysia cared.

Malaysia cared enormously. Malaysia had cared at the United Nations, from the podium, in a voice that trembled. Malaysia had cared at summits, in communiqués and in hashtags. At that very moment the Foreign Ministry had two press releases loaded in the printer, one for a finding of genocide and one for a finding of no genocide. Both opened with the same sentence: *Malaysia has consistently championed the plight of the Rohingya.* A clerk stood beside the printer with a fifty-sen coin, waiting for The Hague.

The judgment never reached the Foreign Ministry. At the airport it met an officer of the Immigration Department.

"Passport?"

The judgment had no passport. It was a foreign document of foreign origin entering without a valid pass, and the Immigration Act 1959/63, drafted with tremendous foresight, knows exactly two kinds of foreigner: those with permission, and the PATI, the Pendatang Asing Tanpa Izin, the foreign arrival without a permit.

There is no third box. There has never been a third box. Malaysia never signed the Refugee Convention, so a person running from a massacre and a backpacker who overstayed a holiday look, to the form, like the same person, and the form has excellent eyesight.

"PATI," said the officer, and filled in the form. Under NAME he wrote the case number. Under STATUS he wrote: *Putusan Asing Tanpa Izin.* A foreign ruling, without a permit.

They took its fingerprints, which was awkward, since it had no fingers, so they inked its staples. They gave it a lanyard and a number. They drove it to a depot.

---

In the depot there was a mat on the floor, and on the mat sat a man called Hamid.

Hamid had a card. It was a good card, laminated, with his photograph and a UN logo. The card was recognised by the United Nations, by the World Court, by the Foreign Ministry's speeches, by the hashtags, and by every government that had ever wept at a podium. It was not recognised by the officer at the roadblock where Hamid had been picked up on his way to a building site, where he worked without a permit, because there was no permit he was allowed to apply for, because the form did not have the box.

"What are you in for?" Hamid asked the judgment.

"I was final and binding," said the judgment.

Hamid nodded. He had heard of those. "I was recognised," he said.

They shared the mat. Across town, the Registrar of Societies was declining, once again, to register a Rohingya association, on the ground that it had never registered one before: the kind of reasoning that, once it starts, never has to stop.

At five o'clock the clerk flipped the coin, the printer printed, and the champion of the Rohingya championed them in a statement that four wire services picked up.

At five past five the officer came round with the evening rice.

"Good news," he told the judgment and the man on the mat. "You are both being held in full accordance with the law."

Then he locked the door, and went to watch Malaysia on the news.
