# Cukai Asap, Not ASAP

The chimney at the steel mill had been called Pakcik Serombong for forty years, and for forty years he had done one thing. He burped. Long, wet, sulphurous burps that rolled over the oil palm and the badminton court and the mamak stall, and settled, as burps do, on the laundry of the people who could not afford dryers.

Nobody charged Pakcik Serombong for his burps. That was the arrangement. In Malaysia, a burp from a chimney was, legally speaking, weather.

Then, on the first of January 2026, a man with a nose arrived in Rotterdam.

He was the Sniffer of Embedded Emissions, an officer of the European Union, and his nose was the size of a Proton Saga. He worked under a regulation called CBAM, which is the sound a coil of steel makes when it is dropped onto a Brussels weighing scale. *CBAM.* He knelt beside a coil of Malaysian hot-rolled steel, unscrewed a small hatch in its side, and inhaled.

"Ah," he said. "Pakcik."

Every coil, it turned out, carried its burp inside it. Not the burp itself, which had long since gone to sit on somebody's laundry, but its ghost: the tonnes of carbon dioxide belched out to make it. The Sniffer could smell the ghost to two decimal places. He wrote the number on an invoice. The invoice was in euros.

"Who has paid for this burp?" he asked.

The coil said nothing, because it was a coil.

"Because if someone in Malaysia has already paid," said the Sniffer, kindly, the way a dentist is kind, "I am allowed to take it off the bill. Article Nine. Carbon price paid in the country of origin. We are very generous."

He waited. Somewhere in Putrajaya, a phone rang and was not answered.

---

The Cabinet was meeting about the weather.

Specifically, it was meeting about global uncertainties, which had been identified as the reason the carbon tax on steel and energy could not, after all, start this year. The minister responsible held a weathervane above his head like a durian he was afraid to drop.

"Should we tax the asap?" asked the Chair.

"Yes," said the minister. "Asap, yes."

"ASAP?"

"No, no. Asap, yes. ASAP, no." The weathervane spun. "The world is not certain yet."

A junior officer at the back, who had studied physics on a scholarship that bonded him to this exact chair for ten years, raised his hand. "With respect, the world has been uncertain since the Big Bang. The Big Bang was extremely uncertain. If we wait for certainty, we will tax carbon at approximately the heat death of the universe."

He was thanked for his input and seconded to the committee on the timeline, where he remains.

The Cabinet agreed on a sequence instead. First a Climate Change Bill. Then a framework for the Bill. Then a policy for the framework. Then a committee to study the timeline of the policy for the framework for the Bill. The Bill was placed on a high shelf, where it began to gather dust. The dust, being fine particulate matter shed by a building air-conditioned to eighteen degrees, was itself an emission, and in Rotterdam the Sniffer's nose twitched.

---

The industry came to say thank you.

They arrived in a convoy of black cars with a cake shaped like a smokestack, iced grey. The chairman of the steelmakers was so round he had his own weather system. He made a speech.

"We thank the government for its wisdom in not taxing our burps during this period of global uncertainty," he said, and to show his gratitude he burped, a magnificent burp, a burp so large it needed a postcode.

Everyone applauded. The cake was cut. Inside, where the sponge should have been, was an envelope from Rotterdam.

The chairman read it and turned the colour of the icing. "But we are not paying the tax," he said. "The tax was deferred."

"Oh, you are paying a tax," said the envelope, which in a story like this one is allowed to talk. "Just not to them."

This was the part nobody in the convoy had read, because it sat on page forty-something of a European regulation and nobody reads page forty-something of anything. The burp gets paid for either way. If Malaysia taxes it, the money stays in Malaysia and fills potholes on the road to the mill. If Malaysia does not, the European importer pays Brussels, the bill rolls back down the chain to the mill, and the money fills potholes in Brussels, which, the chairman was startled to learn, also has potholes.

"So," said the chairman slowly, "we deferred a tax so that we could pay it to the Belgians."

"Not only Belgians," said the envelope. "Some Dutch."

The chairman began to swell. Untaxed smoke, it turns out, does not leave the body. It accumulates. His belly rose like roti canai on a hot griddle, his waistcoat buttons went off like Merdeka fireworks, and with the sound of a lorry tyre giving up on life he lifted off the Cabinet carpet and drifted out of the window, north-west, towards Rotterdam, where the Sniffer stood waiting with a measuring tape and a very small pin.

---

Meanwhile, in a resort in Turkey, the world had gathered to save itself.

Tens of thousands of delegates had flown in to discuss the problem of flying. There was a big top. There were clowns. A young Swedish woman stood at the gate shouting "Circus! Circus!" and the organisers, delighted, gave her a top hat and made her ringmaster.

The European delegation rode in on a white horse made of recycled bottles. They explained that Europe's own chimneys had been handed free permits to burp since 2005, a kindness known as free allocation, and that these free burps would be withdrawn gently, gradually, over the coming decade, and that meanwhile everybody else's burps would be invoiced at the border, in euros, with Article Nine generously available to anyone who already had a carbon price, which very few countries south of the equator had, which was rather the point.

"This is not protectionism," said the European delegate. "This is climate."

"What is the difference?" someone asked.

"The font," said the European, and the tent applauded.

The Malaysian delegation rose next. Their statement said Malaysia remained committed to net zero as early as 2050, that it had launched a National Carbon Market Policy, and that it had deferred its carbon tax with great ambition.

"Great ambition?" asked the ringmaster.

"The greatest," said the delegate. "We are so ambitious about our carbon tax that we refuse to waste it on an uncertain world. We are saving it. Like the good crockery. Like the Musang King. We will bring it out when the guests are worthy."

"And when will that be?"

The delegate checked his weathervane.

---

Back at the mill, Pakcik Serombong burped.

It was a patient burp, the burp of a chimney that has watched ministers come and go and knows that the only permanent institution in the country is the chimney. It rose, and it settled, as before, on the laundry of the people who could not afford dryers. Nobody had consulted them. Nobody ever had. Their lungs had been paying the carbon tax for forty years, at a rate that was never gazetted.

The ghost of the burp slipped into a fresh coil of steel. The coil went onto a ship. The ship sailed for Rotterdam, where the Sniffer of Embedded Emissions, one hand gently deflating a floating Malaysian industrialist with a pin, held out the other for the paperwork.

In Putrajaya, a clerk hung a sign over the counter marked CARBON TAX. It said: TUTUP. KETIDAKPASTIAN GLOBAL.

Ten thousand kilometres away, in Brussels, a new counter opened. Its sign had been written with a translation app, and was therefore perfectly polite.

TERIMA KASIH ATAS ASAP ANDA.

Thank you for your smoke.
