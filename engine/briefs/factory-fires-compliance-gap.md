## ISSUE

On 31 July 2026 in Putrajaya, Fire and Rescue Department (JBPM) director-general Datuk Seri
Nor Hisham Mohammad reported **2,001 factory fires and RM4.21 billion in losses** over six
years. The figure was announced as an enforcement statistic — evidence of a department
tightening its grip on industrial premises.

Read against the department's own numbers, it reads the other way. Factory fires **peaked in
2025 at 463 cases, a 28.6% rise**, the highest in the tracked period, and cost
**RM770.69 million** in that year alone. Enforcement rose in the same year: inspections went
from 1,563 to 1,878, and Fire Hazard Elimination Notices (MBK) from 706 to 1,191 — a 68.6%
jump in notices against a 20% rise in inspections. More inspecting, far more notices, and
more fires.

The developable core is the **denominator**. In 2025 JBPM recorded 16,111 fires nationally
costing RM2.09 billion. Factory fires were **463 of those cases — 2.9% — and RM770.69 million
of the losses, 36.9%**. Under three in a hundred fires, over a third of the money.

The second thread is timing, and it is the one nobody has connected. Section 28 of the Fire
Services Act 1988 requires every designated premises to hold a fire certificate, **renewable
annually**. From **1 January 2026**, JBPM moved that annual renewal to a self-regulated
model: accredited fire safety officers employed on the premises conduct the internal
inspection and file the technical report, replacing the annual JBPM physical inspection, with
departmental audits every two to three years. The department announced this in August 2025 as
a capacity measure. It took effect at the top of the loss curve, and the first six months
under it recorded **222 factory fires**.

**Accuracy posture.** JBPM has not linked the 2025 peak to any cause, and neither may T4A.
The correlation between the accreditation switch and the fire count is **one half-year of
data and is not evidence of causation** — the switch post-dates the 2025 peak it cannot have
caused. The honest frame is a question about sequencing and verification capacity, not an
accusation. Do not assert that self-certification caused fires. Do not assert JBPM concealed
anything: the department published every number used here.

## RELEVANT LAW

- **Fire Services Act 1988 (Act 341)** — verbatim, extracted 2026-08-03 from the AGC LOM
  clean draft dated 7 March 2019 [P1]:

  > **28.** (1) Every designated premises shall require a fire certificate.
  > (2) A fire certificate shall be renewable annually.

  > **33.** Where there is no fire certificate in force in respect of any designated premises
  > the owner of the premises shall be guilty of an offence and shall, on conviction, be
  > liable to a fine not exceeding fifty thousand ringgit or to imprisonment for a term not
  > exceeding five years or to both.

  Section 2 defines **"designated premises"** as "premises the use, size, or location, of
  which has been designated under section 27 for the purpose of issuance of a fire
  certificate". Section 27A requires a fire safety organization in designated premises.
  Sections 29 and 31 give the Director General the issuing power and the fee-setting power;
  section 34 provides rights of appeal.

  **Do not assert that factories are designated premises** on this text alone. The
  designation is made by order under s.27, and that order has not been opened — see
  VERIFICATION GAPS 2. Every secondary description of the scope traces back to fire-safety
  consultancy marketing pages, which are not sources.

- **Fire Services (Amendment) Act 2025 (Act A1764)** — the instrument behind the 1 January
  2026 accreditation regime. **Text not opened** (bomba.gov.my returned 503). The commencement
  date and the mechanism are currently sourced to Bernama reporting the director-general
  [P4], not to the gazette. See VERIFICATION GAPS 1.

- **Fire Services (Code of Conduct of Fire Officer) Regulations 2003, P.U.(A) 388/2003**,
  reprint as at 1 April 2023 [P5] — opened; governs JBPM officer conduct, **not** the
  accredited private officers. Included to record that it was checked and found not to cover
  the accreditation scheme.

- **Occupational Safety and Health Act 1994** — DOSH's parallel industrial-incident
  jurisdiction. Not yet pulled; a possible cross-check on the loss series.

## CONTRADICTIONS

1. **"Since 2020" or "since 2021"?** Malay Mail's headline and body say **since 2020** [S1];
   Bernama's English report frames it as "the past six years" without a start year [P2];
   Sinar Harian's Malay report says **"sejak 2021"** [S2]. Six years counted inclusively from
   2020 ends at 2025; counted from 2021 it ends mid-2026. **Most authoritative: none of them
   yet** — all three are reporting the same spoken statement. **Do not publish a start year.**
   Write "over six years" or "since the start of the period JBPM reported", and if a start
   year is required, get JBPM's annual statistics series first.
2. **"Factory" is narrower than "industrial", and the denominator claim depends on it.**
   [P3] gives industrial fires as 9.2% of 12,292 structural fires ≈ 1,131 cases for 2025;
   [S2] gives 463 factory fires for the same year. These are consistent only if *kilang*
   (factory) is a subset of *industri* (industrial) — almost certainly true, but **not
   verified**. The 2.9%/36.9% denominator claim compares 463 factory cases against the
   **all-fires** total of 16,111, which is a clean comparison and does not depend on this.
   **Never present 463 as the industrial total, and never mix the two series in one sentence.**
3. **The 2024 factory case count is derived, not reported.** 463 ÷ 1.286 ≈ 360. No source
   states 360. If a card needs the 2024 figure, either attribute it explicitly as arithmetic
   from the reported 28.6%, or drop it. Preferred: use the reported percentage, not the
   inferred base.
4. **Case count: 2,001 or 2,002?** Bernama [P2] and Malay Mail [S1] say 2,001; Harian Metro's
   headline says **2,002** [S3, seen via search index only, article not opened]. **Use 2,001**
   — it is the figure on the state wire and in the majority of reports. Note the discrepancy
   rather than silently picking.
5. **Losses do not reconcile to a flat annual rate, and that matters.** RM4.21b over six years
   averages ≈RM702m/year; 2025 alone was RM770.69m, i.e. **above** the six-year average while
   also being the peak case year. Do not describe the loss curve as "flat" or as "rising"
   without the full per-year series — only two years are separately known.

### POST-STAGE-2 ADDITION (2026-08-03): the independent voice, and the parallel withdrawal

Stage 2 returned `bias_score` 15 (BA 85) with two omission flags — institutional monoculture
and **absent human cost** — plus an evasion flag for dodging the conflict of interest in
self-regulation. Searching against those flags surfaced a source that answers all three, and
that materially changes what this issue is about.

**[S7] Malay Mail, 13 July 2026** — *"Worker deaths come at a price: Occupational safety group
calls for workplace inspections to return after Klang factory fire"* —
https://www.malaymail.com/news/malaysia/2026/07/13/worker-deaths-come-at-a-price-occupational-safety-group-calls-for-workplace-inspections-to-return-after-klang-factory-fire/227428
**OPENED.** The **Malaysian Society for Occupational Safety and Health (MSOSH)**, a
professional body outside government, through president Datuk Wira Ahmad Fakhrul Anuar
Ismail:

- Urges the Ministry of Human Resources to **review the need for periodic workplace
  inspections**, describing them as "an important check-and-balance mechanism".
- States that mandatory workplace inspections under the **Factories and Machinery Act 1967
  have been removed**, and that many industries "were still not ready to rely solely on
  **self-regulation**".
- On how self-regulation fails in practice: "procedures exist but are not followed, audits are
  conducted without effective follow-up, and risks are identified but left unmanaged until
  tragedy strikes."
- Notes that **over 90 per cent of Malaysian businesses are SMEs** that may lack internal
  compliance discipline.
- Occasion: a **foreign worker died in a boiler fire at an iron processing plant in Klang on
  Friday 12 July 2026**.

**Why this matters more than filling a gap.** The issue as drafted through Stage 1 v2 was one
regulator moving annual verification in-house. MSOSH describes a **second, separate
withdrawal of periodic inspection by a different regulator** — DOSH under the Ministry of
Human Resources — in the same period, and says the industrial base is not ready for it. Two
inspection regimes stepping back at once is a materially stronger and better-evidenced story
than one, and it comes with an independent professional body on the record against it, which
is precisely the voice the sourcing lacked.

**Handle with care.** JBPM's fire-certificate regime and DOSH's Factories and Machinery Act
inspections are **different statutes, different ministries, different duties**. Do not merge
them into a single "the inspectors left" claim. The defensible framing is that two separate
verification regimes moved toward self-regulation over the same period, attributed to MSOSH
for the DOSH half.

**[S8] DOSH / DOSM occupational injury statistics, 2023** (most recent located): 38,950
occupational injury cases nationally, up 13.8 per cent on 2022; 324 fatal occupational
injuries; manufacturing 10,335 injuries and **66 fatalities**, second only to construction.
Not fire-specific and three years stale, so usable only as scale context, never as a
factory-fire casualty figure.

**Still missing after this addition:** an FMM or manufacturer response, a union/MTUC voice,
an insurer's independent loss series, and any JBPM or DOSH figure for **deaths or injuries in
factory fires specifically**. Stage 2 is right that the human cost is absent; this addition
supplies one named death and a sector-level fatality count, not the series.
